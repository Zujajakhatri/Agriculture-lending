Agricultural credit disbursement by the 39 banks in our dataset more than doubled, from PKR 1,183.8 billion in 2020 to PKR 2,542.6 billion in 2025, a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 16.5%. The market grew every year:

Growth slowed sharply in 2022 (3.7%), then accelerated to around 25% a year in 2023 and 2024, before moderating to 16.4% in 2025.

HBL's role

Habib Bank Limited (HBL) is the market's largest agricultural lender, disbursing PKR 455.6 billion in 2025, a 17.9% market share. Its lending grew every year, from PKR 186.2 billion in 2020 (a CAGR of about 19.6%), with the biggest jump in 2025 (+34%).

Disbursement vs outstanding loans

Outstanding agricultural loans (gross advances) grew from PKR 669 billion to PKR 1,328 billion (CAGR 14.7%), slightly slower than disbursement. In 2024, gross advances fell 6.1% while disbursement rose 25.3%. Agriculture's share of total bank advances moved from 7.45% in 2020 to 7.03% in 2025, with a low of 5.78% in 2024, while total advances grew at 16.0% a year.

Agricultural disbursement is growing at roughly the same pace as overall bank lending, so agriculture is not gaining a larger place on bank balance sheets. Most agri lending is short-term seasonal credit that is repaid within the year.

Note: Disbursement figures are from the State Bank of Pakistan on a fiscal-year basis (e.g. 2025 = July 2024–June 2025). All figures are nominal; Pakistan experienced high inflation during 2022–2024, so real growth is lower than these figures suggest.

Conventional and Islamic commercial banks dominate, providing 78.5% of agricultural disbursement in 2025, broadly stable since 2020 (76.5%). The market is not highly concentrated: the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) is about 870, below the 1,500 threshold for moderate concentration. Still, the top 5 lenders hold 56.7% and the top 10 hold 77.3% of disbursement.

3 Where is financing demand concentrated?

Province-wise lending data is not available, so demand is measured through proxies: number of farms, agricultural workforce, cultivated area and crop output.

  • Punjab is the core of demand. It leads on every indicator and produces 56% of national crop output.
  • Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has a distinct profile. It holds 37.1% of the country's farms (4.48 million) on only 6.3% of the cultivated area. These are very small farms that need small loans, a segment that is costly to serve through branches.
  • Sindh ties with KP on the Demand Index and dominates fisheries (57% of national output), a niche that the Risk Coverage Scheme now explicitly covers.
  • Balochistan has the highest agricultural share of provincial GDP (25%), but its farm base is small and its farms are large, which limits the scale of the market.
  • Crops are highly concentrated: the top 5 crops cover 82.9% of cultivated area. Wheat alone is 47.1%, followed by rice (16.5%) and cotton (9.5%), so crop lending follows the Rabi (wheat) and Kharif (rice, cotton) seasons.

In 2025, average agricultural credit was about PKR 210,400 per farm and PKR 41,600 per cultivated acre.

4 Where can a Digital Retail Bank participate?

The DRB Opportunity Index scores each province from 0 to 100 using six equally weighted factors: agricultural employees, agriculture's share of GDP, agriculture's share of employment, number of farms, small farm size and cultivated area. Levels: High ≥ 70, Medium 40–70, Low < 40.

RankProvinceDRB Opportunity Score (2025)Level
1Punjab85.5High
2Khyber Pakhtunkhwa67.7Medium
3Sindh51.4Medium
4Balochistan36.2Low

Punjab is the only High-opportunity province and offers the largest market, but it is also the most competitive, with commercial banks, the Bank of Punjab and PPCBL already active. A DRB would need a clear point of difference here, such as speed, a digital journey and input financing through merchants.

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is the most natural fit for a digital model. Its large number of very small farms matches the small-ticket, low-cost lending that digital channels do best. Its score of 67.7 is just below the High threshold, and it has been rising since 2020 as the number of farms grew and average farm size fell. Under the Risk Coverage Scheme, farmers of all landholding sizes in KP are eligible for the 10% first-loss cover, which further reduces lending risk there.

Sindh is a moderate opportunity, with fisheries and crop finance as possible niches. Balochistan is a longer-term option: Risk Coverage Scheme support is broad, but the market is small.

The small-ticket segment is under-served. Microfinance and digital banks hold only 9.9% of disbursement (PKR 252.6 billion) across 11 banks, averaging PKR 23.0 billion per bank, compared with PKR 81.8 billion for the other 28 banks. Their share has fallen from 11.8% in 2020, and they grew more slowly (CAGR 12.7% vs 17.0%), which leaves room for a new digital lender.

5 Opportunity for Digital Banks

Microfinance banks have demonstrated that small-ticket agricultural lending can be commercially viable. Digital retail banks (DRBs) can build on this model while reducing the cost of serving farmers through digital onboarding, satellite-enabled verification, alternative data, and merchant-led disbursement. This can make agricultural financing more scalable, accessible, and cost-efficient, particularly for small and underserved farmers.

Highest-potential products

1

Digital Crop Loans

Small-ticket, seasonal financing designed around farmers' crop cycles, with digital application, approval, and repayment.

2

Input Financing

Digital financing for essential agricultural inputs, including seeds, fertilizer, pesticides, and diesel, disbursed directly through a network of verified merchants.

3

Livestock Finance

Small-ticket livestock financing bundled with appropriate insurance coverage to reduce both farmer and lender risk.

4

Post-Harvest Finance

Short-term financing against stored agricultural produce, supported by warehouse receipts, enabling farmers to delay sales until market conditions improve.

5

Value-Chain Finance

An integrated financing model connecting farmers, aggregators, buyers, input suppliers, merchants, and the bank, allowing financing to flow across the agricultural value chain.
